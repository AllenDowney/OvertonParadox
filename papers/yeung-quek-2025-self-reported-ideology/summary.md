## Design

This is a national Lucid survey from March 2022, with quota sampling to match national benchmarks on gender, age, race and income (n = 3828; p. 416). As preregistered, the authors dropped 529 "speeders," 12.1% of the original sample (p. 419). Respondents were randomly assigned to one of three measures (pp. 416–417):

1. **Control:** the standard ANES 7-point liberal–conservative question.
2. **Add Definitions:** the same question, preceded by textbook definitions from Lowi et al. (2019). Liberals favor an active government role in "supporting social and political change." Conservatives want government to "uphold traditional values" with intervention "as little as possible."
3. **Subtract Labels:** the same definitions, but without the words "liberal" and "conservative." The ends of the scale are "Government plays strong and active role" (1) and "Government intervenes as little as possible" (7), and option 4 is "Middle of the road."

Respondents in Subtract Labels later answered the standard question too, which allows a within-subjects comparison (p. 417). A four-item quiz measured "ideological knowledge" (α = 0.65; p. 418).

## Main results

- **People don't know the textbook meanings.** The mean is 1.94 correct out of 4 (p. 419). Only 36% get three or more right, and 18% get all four (p. 419). Moderates score lower than non-moderates (1.30 vs 2.31; p. 419). Democrats, Black respondents, women and less sophisticated respondents also score lower (p. 421).
- **No aggregate effect.** The means are 4.12 (control), 4.02 (Add Definitions) and 4.08 (Subtract Labels), with no difference significant at 10% (p. 423). Adding definitions reduces the share choosing moderate and raises the variance from 2.77 to 3.22 (p. 423).
- **Partisans move toward the middle.** Without the labels, Republicans move 0.5 points more liberal and Democrats 0.6 points more conservative, relative to Independents (p. 424; Table 2, p. 425). The share of Republicans at 5–7 falls from 71% to 49% (p. 425). Both parties' modal response becomes 4 (p. 425). The changes "cancel out" in the aggregate (p. 425).
- **Within subjects:** Democrats move +0.59 and Republicans −0.49 (p. 426). The Democrat–Republican gap shrinks from 2.12 to 1.04 points, "half the gap" (p. 426).
- **Sorting:** the ideology–party correlation is r = 0.58 with the labels and 0.26 without them (p. 428). The paper's conclusion is that self-placement is "endogenous to their party identification" (p. 429).

## Context the paper gives

- It distinguishes operational from symbolic ideology, citing Simas, Ellis & Stimson, and Popp & Rudolph (pp. 412–413).
- It cites work showing that elites can reorient what the labels mean: Amira (2022), and Hopkins & Noel (2022), "Trump and the shifting meaning of 'conservative'" (p. 414).
- "Moderate" and "conservative" are socially desirable labels and "liberal" has negative connotations. Self-identified conservatives outnumber liberals although policy views are generally liberal (Ellis & Stimson; p. 415). Zell & Bernstein (2014) find that Americans "systematically overestimate their level of conservatism" (p. 415).
- For contrary evidence it cites de Abreu Maia (2021) and Fowler et al. (2023): self-placement "meaningfully captures policy attitudes" (note 7, p. 415). It does not engage with Kollman & Jackson or any argument about change over time.

## Caveats/limitations

- One opt-in online sample at one point in time. There are no trends and no analysis by age.
- The label-free item is essentially a scale of the government's role. The authors note that it improved the correlation with preferences about government responsibility but "reduced the correlation ... for fiscal policy" (p. 428).
- They "do not assert" that the label-free measure is "more reflective of reality" (p. 428). The results may depend on the definitions chosen (p. 429).
- Expressive responding and the "Don't know" option may affect the knowledge results (p. 423).

## Relation to this project

**Supports the distinction behind our measure.** The paper gives causal evidence that polviews mixes policy content with party and identity content. That is a reason to measure conservatism from 53 issue questions rather than from the label. The authors recommend IRT on policy preferences, such as Caughey et al. and Fowler et al., when the goal is operational ideology (p. 430).

**Weak on relativity.** The experiment changes the wording, not the reference point. It doesn't show that labels are judged relative to a center, a moving center, or the people around you. The closest material is cited, not tested: partisans see opponents as extreme, so ideologues call themselves "moderate" (Hare et al.; p. 415), and elites can shift what "conservative" means (p. 414). Hopkins & Noel and Amira might be worth a look for the moving-meaning argument.

**Nothing on age.** Age is used only for quotas (p. 416).

**Aggregate mean unchanged.** This matters for us. Removing the labels does not shift the population mean (p. 423), so the paper does not suggest that the GSS mean of polviews is biased by the labels at a given time. Its effects concern how partisans spread out on the scale.

**Kollman & Jackson.** The paper does not engage with them. Its finding that partisans' self-placement is inflated by identity could fit with "more informative over time": if the labels track party more closely as sorting rises, self-placement predicts vote better without becoming more operational. That is our inference, not a claim the paper makes.

## Possible use

- **Introduction:** one sentence, with Ellis & Stimson, saying that self-placement partly measures symbolic, party-linked identity, for example the gap halving without labels (p. 426).
- **Methods:** optionally, cite their recommendation of IRT policy scales for operational ideology (p. 430) to support our latent score.
- **Not** as evidence for relative labels or age effects.
