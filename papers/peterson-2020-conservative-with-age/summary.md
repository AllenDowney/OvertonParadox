## Design

A **panel** study of a single cohort: the Michigan Youth-Parent Socialization Panel Study (MSS). It started with 1,669 high school seniors (and their parents) in 1965. The seniors were reinterviewed in 1973, 1982 and 1997, so the data cover ages 18 to about 50. In 1997, 935 were reinterviewed, "an impressive retention rate of 56%" (p. 604). The authors note the sample is not fully representative, because many students of the time dropped out before senior year (p. 604).

The measures are seven-point items (1 = most liberal, 7 = most conservative): party identification (all four waves), and, from 1973 on, self-reported ideology, government aid to minorities, equality for women, marijuana legalization, guaranteed jobs, and rights of the accused (p. 605).

Three analyses:

1. **Aggregate means** by wave, with t-tests between waves (Table 1).
2. **Comparison with the ANES**, to separate aging from the national trend, for the four items with matching ANES questions: the full ANES sample, ANES respondents the same age as the MSS cohort, and ANES respondents always aged 21–29 (Table 2).
3. **Individual-level change**: transitions among liberal/moderate/conservative from 1973 to 1997 (Table 3), and growth-mixture models with up to five latent classes (stay conservative, stay liberal, stay moderate, move conservative, move liberal), chosen by Vuong-Lo-Mendell-Rubin tests (Table 4).

Their hypotheses (p. 602): attitudes are mostly stable across adulthood, and any change is more likely to be rightward than leftward.

## Main results

**Absolute change (Table 1, p. 605).** Six of seven items moved significantly right as the cohort aged from 26 to 50. The exception was equality for women, which moved left by 1.05. "Typical movement to the right on the six variables is around a half point on the seven-point scales, with a range from .35 on legalizing marijuana to .92 for providing rights for the accused" (p. 605). Ideology went from 3.78 (1973) to 4.23 (1982) to 4.33 (1997); guaranteed jobs from 4.35 to 4.86 to 4.78.

**Mostly in one decade.** "In all cases the lion's share of this movement came between age 26 and 35 (i.e., from 1973 to 1982)" (p. 605). From 35 to 50, only party ID (+.15) and ideology (+.10) moved right significantly; on the five issue items there was no significant rightward change, and the cohort became slightly more liberal on women and on rights of the accused (pp. 605–606). The authors suggest the 1973–1982 shift may be a period effect (the Reagan era), and say that "longitudinal data with a single cohort cannot separate aging from period effects" (p. 606).

**Relative to the country (Table 2, pp. 606–607).** Party ID: the MSS cohort was .22 more Republican than the ANES in 1965 and .28 more in 1997, so almost no relative shift. Ideology: 3.78 vs. 4.14 in 1973 (significantly more liberal than the nation), 4.33 vs. 4.33 in 1997. Minority assistance: .44 more liberal in 1973, .30 more liberal in 1997. Women's equality: the cohort moved liberal by 1.05 (2.99 to 1.94), the nation by 1.27 (3.51 to 2.24). Overall, relative differences "are typically only about .2 or .3 on a scale of 1–7" (p. 607). Against the "forever-young" ANES subgroup aged 21–29, only ideology showed a growing gap: by 1997 the MSS cohort was significantly more conservative (4.33 vs. 3.98) (p. 607).

**Individual transitions in ideology (Table 3, p. 608).** From 1973 to 1997, 44% of liberals stayed liberal, 48% of moderates stayed moderate, and 67% of conservatives stayed conservative. 34% of liberals became conservatives, but only 19% of conservatives became liberals. Moderates who changed were three times as likely to become conservative (39%) as liberal (13%). Overall: 51% no change, 28% moved to conservative, 10% to liberal, 11% to moderate.

**Growth-mixture models (Table 4, pp. 608–609).** Averaged over the seven items, about 75% of the cohort is in a "stable" class, 17.4% in "move conservative" and 7% in "move liberal". For ideology, about 21% move conservative and 4% move liberal. Guaranteed jobs is "the big exception" (p. 608): the best model has only two classes, stay conservative (77.5%) and move liberal (22.5%).

**Conclusion (p. 609).** The conservative drift for party ID and minority assistance "was largely accounted for by changes in the national mood", while views on women became more liberal. Because the variable that changes most is the self-label, not issues, "one intriguing possibility is that aging may increase the likelihood of identifying as a conservative more than it leads people actually to be conservative" (p. 609). They cite Ellis and Stimson (2012) on the gap between operational and symbolic ideology, and say they know of no evidence that this gap grows with age.

## Context the paper gives

- **Why cross-sections mislead (p. 603):** "Just because older people are more conservative than younger people at any given time does not mean that people have become more conservative as they age." Possible reasons include differential mortality, differential survey dropout, and "Perhaps society itself became more liberal over time" (citing Schwadel and Garneau 2014).
- **Synthetic cohorts (p. 603):** these assume within-cohort change comes from aging, not attrition, and the authors point to "recent evidence of systematic differences in mortality rates for liberals and conservatives" (Pabayo et al. 2015; Subramanian et al. 2009, 2010).
- **The relative-position argument, stated directly (p. 605):** if older people "have merely continued to hold the beliefs they did when they were 20 years old—beliefs that decades later might appear to be conservative—then the apparent conservatizing effects of age would be misleading."
- **Literature (pp. 601–602):** political science models (lifelong persistence, impressionable years, party loyalty, lifelong openness, running tally) concern stability, not direction; work in psychology and sociology expects rightward change. They note that the case for conservative drift "is not equally strong" for social and economic issues (p. 602).

## Caveats/limitations

- One cohort (born around 1947) that came of age in the 1960s; the authors ask whether results would differ for another cohort (p. 609). With one cohort, aging and period effects are confounded.
- Ages 18–50 only; nothing about later life (pp. 604, 609).
- Few items; the ANES baseline exists for only four. Simple t-tests of means.
- 56% retention, and the sample excludes high school dropouts.
- The transition analysis collapses a 7-point scale into three categories, so some movement could be measurement noise. In the growth-mixture models (Table A.2), the ideology "move conservative" class starts at 3.42 and the "move liberal" class at 4.90, a pattern that regression toward the middle could also produce. Some "stable" classes have significant slopes (e.g., stay-conservative on guaranteed jobs, +.299), so the class labels are approximate.

## Relation to this project

**Supports.** The paper's main message agrees with ours: the cross-sectional age gradient does not show that individuals move right, and a society that moves liberal can make older people look conservative (pp. 603, 605). Over the full span, issue positions are mostly stable, and the remaining drift relative to the nation is small, about .2–.3 points on a 1–7 scale.

**The Overton paradox in their own numbers.** Their women's-equality item is our pattern in miniature: the cohort moved liberal in absolute terms (−1.05) but less than the country (−1.27), while its self-reported ideology moved from more liberal than the nation (3.78 vs. 4.14) to equal (4.33 vs. 4.33), and became more conservative than young adults. They speculate that aging makes people *identify* as conservative more than it makes them conservative (p. 609). Our account explains the same finding without aging: the self-label is relative to a moving center. A paper on our results can present the Overton paradox as an answer to the open question they raise.

**Complicates.** In absolute terms their cohort moved right on six of seven items, mostly from 1973 to 1982, while we find a slow liberal drift within cohorts. These are consistent, not contradictory: our 1950s birth decade "moved a little toward conservative until about 1980, and toward liberal after that" (post 1), and the MSS cohort, born in the late 1940s, was observed mostly over that rightward period. For a single cohort, neither their panel nor our model can separate aging from period effects; our model uses many cohorts, which is the evidence they say is missing.

**Panel vs. cross-section.** They follow the same individuals; we follow birth years in repeated cross-sections. Their point about differential mortality (p. 603) is a real threat to our "changing minds" interpretation: if members of a cohort who die earlier differ in conservatism, cohort averages could drift with no individual change. We should address it; their panel, where it overlaps our period, is a partial check.

**Issue domains.** Weak connection. They note that social and economic issues might differ (p. 602) but do not test it. Their only economic item, guaranteed jobs, moved right from 1973 to 1982 (4.35 to 4.86) with no ANES baseline, and its growth-mixture model has no "move conservative" class. It neither confirms nor contradicts our finding that core economic questions move conservative within cohorts.

## Possible use

- **Introduction:** the main panel evidence on whether people become more conservative with age, and the argument that cross-sectional age gradients confound aging with period change (p. 603).
- **Methods/limitations:** when we justify repeated cross-sections with a period–cohort model instead of a panel, and when we discuss differential mortality and attrition.
- **Discussion:** their finding that self-reported ideology moves more than issue positions, and their speculation about why (p. 609), answered by the Overton paradox. Their women's-equality numbers (Table 2) are a clean example to quote.
- Recommendation: cite in all three places; the discussion is the most important.
