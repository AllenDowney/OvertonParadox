## Design

This is a "perspective article" with two aims: to "descriptively present public opinion trends about individual and national welfare," and to show that such trends exist only because of NSF-funded infrastructure (introduction). The data come from the ANES (started 1948) and the GSS (started 1972, "fully adopted probability methods by 1977").

The **Survey sampling** section defends probability samples. GSS response rates were "75%, 70%, and 45%" in 1976, 2000, and 2024, and the ANES rates were "70%, 61%, and 33%." The authors argue that low response rates are "technically orthogonal to biased estimation." What matters is whether nonresponse is systematic. They concede that the ANES "mispredicted the presidential vote in 2024 by roughly 5.7% points." Against opt-in panels they raise unknown selection, the problem of choosing quotas, and bogus or AI respondents. They add that switching sample type midway would make any change over time ambiguous.

The analysis is deliberately simple (**Results**). Annual means ± SD use the provided weights, with modes merged in the main text and face-to-face-only results in the supplement ("the general trends remain the same"). Subgroups by party, race, and sex are in the supplement. The authors say they "chose these trends prior to fully knowing what all of them would show" (**Discussion**).

## Main results

- **Individual welfare (stable):** Perceived relative family income had a mean of 2.92 in both 1972 and 2024. Financial satisfaction dips with economic shocks. Health and happiness have declined slightly since about 2020; happiness went from 2.19 (2018) to 2.03 (2024). The authors call all of these shifts "minor."
- **National welfare (declining):** Satisfaction with democracy fell between 2008 and 2012. Affective polarization rose by "roughly 30-percentage-point[s]" since 2000, and the out-party rating fell from 41.01 to 19.27. External efficacy (ANES) fell from 67.82 (1952) to 46.79 (2000) to 22.12 (2024). GSS confidence fell for banks, Congress, education, medicine, organized religion, the press, and television, with "very recent sharp drops" for science and the Supreme Court.
- **Subgroups:** In the 1970s, the only clear partisan gaps in confidence were for major companies and organized labor. Now Democrats have more confidence in education, medicine, the press, science, and television, and Republicans have more in the military, organized religion, and the Supreme Court (**Subgroups**).

## Context the paper gives

- In **Implications of trends**, the authors link low efficacy and trust to right-wing populism and possibly to political violence, and they flag this as "speculating."
- The **Discussion** offers affective polarization and happiness as examples of research fields that grew out of trend data.
- Its references include Hout (2021), "America's liberal social climate and trends: change in 283 general social survey variables between and within US birth cohorts, 1972–2018" (ref. 5). The paper cites it only as a review.

## Caveats/limitations

- The trends are "selected," and the analysis is descriptive with no modeling (**Discussion**).
- The "baseline" may be artificial, because "the second half of the 20th century was unusually salubrious."
- The mode change (GSS online from 2021) is handled only by a supplementary check.
- Druckman is co-PI of a large nonprobability project, which he discloses.

## Relation to this project

**Weak.** The paper reports nothing on ideology, polviews, liberal/conservative identification, social or moral attitudes, or age, cohort, or generation. The only mention of ideology contrasts affective polarization with "conventional treatments of polarization that focus on ideology or issue positions." The only mention of age is a note that it ignores practices such as excluding respondents under 25 from happiness analyses. Its subgroups are party, race, and sex.

Nothing in it supports or contradicts our results. Two minor points touch our methods:

- Its warning about mode effects since the 2021 online switch applies to our 2021–2024 data.
- Its exclusion of the executive from confidence items, because confidence in the executive "largely is a proxy for partisanship," parallels in spirit our exclusion of confidence questions. Our reason was different, though: those questions evaluate institutions rather than taking positions on issues.

## Possible use

- **Introduction or data section (optional):** one sentence on the value of long-running probability surveys, for example that such series "are impossible to recreate once the trend line stops" (**Discussion**). This is the use the outside reviewer suggested. It isn't needed.
- **Follow the reference instead:** Hout (2021, *Public Opinion Quarterly* 85:1009–1049) is directly relevant to between- and within-cohort liberal change in the GSS, and we should check and cite it.
