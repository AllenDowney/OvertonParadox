Reparameterizing the RW2 Prior

A technical memo on a faster parameterization of the same model

November 3, 2026

Reparameterizing the RW2 prior¶

The period–cohort model in this project fits two second-order random walks (RW2): one over birth years and one over survey years. Fitted with NUTS in its usual form, almost every draw needs about 1,000 leapfrog steps. This memo describes a change of variables that leaves the model unchanged but cuts the time per fit by 6–20×:

  1. write the RW2 prior in the eigenbasis of its covariance matrix;

  2. split the walk’s linear trend out as a separate coordinate.

The prior, the likelihood, and the posterior over everything the model reports stay the same. Only the coordinates the sampler moves in change.

The code is rw2(..., form="trend") in overton/apc.py. The check that both forms are the same model is overton/rw2_trend.py, and the experiments are in docs/lab.md.

The model¶

The model is CultureWar’s model10 as ported to overton/apc.py. Each cohort × year cell has a weighted mean score yˉct\bar y_{ct}yˉ​ct​, observed with standard error σtresid/ncteff\sigma^{\text{resid}}_t / \sqrt{n^{\text{eff}}_{ct}}σtresid​/ncteff​​, around

logit⁡−1(α+fccohort+ftperiod+ϵt),\operatorname{logit}^{-1}\left(\alpha + f^{\text{cohort}}_c + f^{\text{period}}_t + \epsilon_t\right),logit−1(α+fccohort​+ftperiod​+ϵt​),
(1)

where ϵ\epsilonϵ is zero-sum year noise. With the p48 question set, there are 2,442 cells, 124 birth years, 53 survey years, and 284 free parameters.

Each walk fff of length nnn is a mean-centered RW2, built by double cumulative sums with a free initial slope:

z∼N(0,In−2),v0∼N(0,0.22),f=σ PLz+v0 r.z \sim \mathcal N(0, I_{n-2}), \qquad v_0 \sim \mathcal N(0, 0.2^2), \qquad f = \sigma\, P L z + v_0\, r .z∼N(0,In−2​),v0​∼N(0,0.22),f=σPLz+v0​r.
(2)

  • LLL is the n×(n−2)n \times (n-2)n×(n−2) double-cumsum map from innovations to levels, with fraw,0=0f_{\text{raw},0} = 0fraw,0​=0 and the first slope 0. Row kkk of LLL is (k−1,k−2,…,1,0,…,0)(k-1, k-2, \ldots, 1, 0, \ldots, 0)(k−1,k−2,…,1,0,…,0).

  • P=I−1n11⊤P = I - \tfrac1n \mathbf 1 \mathbf 1^\topP=I−n1​11⊤ removes the mean.

  • r=P (0,1,…,n−1)⊤r = P\,(0, 1, \ldots, n-1)^\topr=P(0,1,…,n−1)⊤ is the mean-centered ramp, which the initial slope v0v_0v0​ scales.

  • σ\sigmaσ is the walk’s scale. It has an exponential prior.

This is the standard non-centered form. The sampler moves in (z,v0,log⁡σ)(z, v_0, \log\sigma)(z,v0​,logσ).

Why it is slow¶

NUTS with a diagonal mass matrix can rescale each coordinate, but it can’t rotate them. When the posterior is strongly correlated in the sampler’s coordinates, trajectories need many small steps.

On the saved draws of the decided p48 fit, the posterior’s correlation matrix in the sampler’s coordinates has a condition number of about 9,200. Its smallest-variance directions are smooth shapes of the cohort curve over the data-rich birth years:

  • Many coordinates per shape. The data pin down each smooth shape well. But in innovation coordinates, a smooth shape is a weighted sum of about 50 entries of zzz, so the posterior is narrow along a direction that no single coordinate represents.

  • A funnel. The walk is σ\sigmaσ times the innovations, so each well-identified shape also couples zzz to σ\sigmaσ.

  • The ramp twice over. The smoothest of these shapes is nearly the linear ramp rrr, which v0v_0v0​ also scales. So v0v_0v0​ and the smoothest innovations trade off against each other.

As a result, the production fit (nutpie, 4 chains × (4000 + 6000), target_accept 0.99) reached the maximum tree depth of 10 on every draw. That’s 1,023 gradient evaluations per draw.

Step 1: the eigenbasis¶

Given σ\sigmaσ, the walk without the slope term, σPLz\sigma P L zσPLz, is Gaussian with covariance σ2PLL⊤P\sigma^2 P L L^\top Pσ2PLL⊤P. Take its eigendecomposition and keep the n−2n-2n−2 nonzero eigenvalues:

PLL⊤P=UΛU⊤,M=UΛ1/2(n×(n−2)).P L L^\top P = U \Lambda U^\top, \qquad M = U \Lambda^{1/2} \quad (n \times (n-2)).PLL⊤P=UΛU⊤,M=UΛ1/2(n×(n−2)).
(3)

Now define

w=Qz,Q=M+PL,w = Q z, \qquad Q = M^{+} P L ,w=Qz,Q=M+PL,
(4)

where M+M^{+}M+ is the pseudoinverse. Since MMM has full column rank and (PL)(PL)⊤=MM⊤(PL)(PL)^\top = M M^\top(PL)(PL)⊤=MM⊤, QQQ is square and orthogonal, and PLz=MwPLz = MwPLz=Mw. So:

  • w∼N(0,I)w \sim \mathcal N(0, I)w∼N(0,I), exactly as zzz is;

  • the change of variables has Jacobian 1;

  • f=σMw+v0rf = \sigma M w + v_0 rf=σMw+v0​r is the same walk.

Each column of MMM is one shape of the walk, from smoothest (largest λ\lambdaλ) to roughest. A smooth shape the data pin down is now a single coordinate wkw_kwk​, and the mass matrix can scale it.

On its own, this roughly doubled the ESS per minute (with TyMC and PyMC-style adaptation). But with nutpie-style adaptation, a few coordinates mixed badly (ESS 10–20). Those were the smoothest shapes and v0v_0v0​.

Step 2: split out the trend¶

The smoothest columns of MMM are close to the ramp rrr, so v0v_0v0​ and the leading wkw_kwk​ explain nearly the same thing: the walk’s overall linear trend. The fix is to make that trend a coordinate of its own. Decompose MMM into its projection on rrr and the rest:

a=M⊤rr⊤r,M⊥=(I−rr⊤r⊤r)M,M=ra⊤+M⊥.a = \frac{M^\top r}{r^\top r}, \qquad M_\perp = \left(I - \frac{r r^\top}{r^\top r}\right) M, \qquad M = r a^\top + M_\perp .a=r⊤rM⊤r​,M⊥​=(I−r⊤rrr⊤​)M,M=ra⊤+M⊥​.
(5)

Then

f=σM⊥w+β r,β=v0+σ a⊤w.f = \sigma M_\perp w + \beta\, r, \qquad \beta = v_0 + \sigma\, a^\top w .f=σM⊥​w+βr,β=v0​+σa⊤w.
(6)

β\betaβ is the walk’s total linear trend, and M⊥wM_\perp wM⊥​w has no linear component. The map (v0,w)↦(β,w)(v_0, w) \mapsto (\beta, w)(v0​,w)↦(β,w) is a shear with Jacobian 1. So the same model is:

  • w∼N(0,I)w \sim \mathcal N(0, I)w∼N(0,I);

  • β\betaβ with a flat prior;

  • v0v_0v0​’s prior, N(0,0.22)\mathcal N(0, 0.2^2)N(0,0.22), added as a potential evaluated at v0=β−σa⊤wv_0 = \beta - \sigma a^\top wv0​=β−σa⊤w.

The data identify β\betaβ directly, as the trend of the curve, and in these coordinates it no longer competes with the smooth shapes.

Code¶

The basis is computed once in numpy. In PyMC (overton/apc.py):

def rw2_trend_basis(n):
    L = np.zeros((n, n - 2))
    for k in range(2, n):
        L[k, : k - 1] = np.arange(k - 1, 0, -1)
    P = np.eye(n) - 1 / n
    lam, U = np.linalg.eigh(P @ L @ L.T @ P)
    keep = lam > 1e-9 * lam.max()
    M = U[:, keep] * np.sqrt(lam[keep])
    r = P @ np.arange(n, dtype=float)
    a = M.T @ r / (r @ r)
    return M - np.outer(r, a), r, a


# inside rw2(name, n, sigma, slope_init_scale, dims, form="trend")
M_perp, r, a = rw2_trend_basis(n)
w = pm.Normal(f"{name}_w", 0.0, 1.0, shape=n - 2)
beta = pm.Flat(f"{name}_trend")
v0 = beta - sigma * pm.math.dot(a, w)
pm.Potential(f"{name}_slope_init_prior", pm.logp(pm.Normal.dist(0.0, slope_init_scale), v0))
f = pm.Deterministic(name, sigma * pm.math.dot(M_perp, w) + beta * r, dims=dims)

The TyMC version, used by the browser page, is rw2_trend in overtron/spike/model.ts.

Checking that it is the same model¶

python -m overton.rw2_trend builds the p48 period–cohort and period models both ways. It draws random points in the current coordinates and maps them to the new ones with w=Qzw = Qzw=Qz and β=v0+σa⊤w\beta = v_0 + \sigma a^\top wβ=v0​+σa⊤w. Then it compares the log densities, including the Jacobians of the transforms. They agree to about 10-9. The TyMC port agrees with PyMC to about 2.5×10−72.5 \times 10^{-7}2.5×10−7, the precision of PyMC’s float32 constants.

What didn’t help¶

  • Centering. Sampling the levels of the walk directly made the conditioning worse by 35–175×, as screened on the saved draws (not sampled). Neighboring levels of a smooth curve are nearly collinear. In CultureWar’s period-only time model on binomial series, centering did help, so the answer depends on the model and the data.

  • Moving the slope’s anchor. Making the free slope the one at a middle birth year, instead of the first, didn’t change the conditioning.

Caveats¶

  • Threads. The trend form multiplies by a dense n×(n−2)n \times (n-2)n×(n−2) matrix in every gradient. With default settings, BLAS may start its own threads in every chain. But the product is only about 15,000 multiply-adds, too small to be worth splitting across threads. Set OMP_NUM_THREADS=1 OPENBLAS_NUM_THREADS=1 MKL_NUM_THREADS=1.

    • Laptop (12 cores): p48 period–cohort fit, 4 × (1000 + 1000). One thread per chain took 25 s on about 3 cores. The default took 56 s with 70 threads on about 10 cores.

    • Unity (AMD EPYC 7402): the same fit at production length, after a warm-up, each configuration twice. With 4 or 16 cores and one or default threads, every run took 109–115 s.

    So the setting helps on one machine and costs nothing on the other. Extra cores don’t speed up a single fit either: put them into more chains or more fits.

  • Prior predictive. pm.Flat and pm.Potential can’t be forward-sampled. Draw prior predictive samples from the current form, which is the same model.

  • Scope. The derivation covers the Gaussian RW2 with a free initial slope. A Student-t RW2 isn’t Gaussian, so the eigenbasis argument doesn’t apply. A walk without the free slope has no v0v_0v0​ to absorb a⊤wa^\top wa⊤w, so it would need its own derivation.

  • Default since 2026-11-03. New fits use the trend form, and --rw2-form cumsum selects the current form. Results fitted before then used the current form. Summaries record which form was used.
